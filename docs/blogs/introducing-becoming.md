# Introducing Becoming

**Record what you do. Shape who you become.**

Becoming is a personal life management app.

It is a place where you can put the different pieces of your life together:

* a casual idea that suddenly comes to mind,
* an ambiguous goal you are still trying to understand,
* a specific task you need to do,
* an important decision you have made,
* a thought or feeling you want to remember,
* a note containing something you have learned,
* and an honest record of what you actually did and how it went.

But Becoming is not just a place to store these things.

You can connect them.

An idea can become a goal.
A goal can lead to a project.
A project can generate tasks.
Doing those tasks creates records.
Those records may cause you to change your plan, make a decision, or write down something you learned.

Over time, these connections form a map of your life.

You can look at that map and ask:

**Where have I been?**

**Where am I now?**

**Where am I trying to go?**

**Why did I choose this direction?**

**Am I still going in the right direction?**

# Why I Built Becoming

I have tried many task-management and todo apps.

Most of them are good at answering one question:

**What things have I not completed yet?**

But I gradually realized that this is not the question I struggle with most.

Imagine an ordinary afternoon.

I open my todo list.

There are twenty things waiting for me.

Which one should I do?

Maybe I should choose the highest-priority task.

But several of them are marked high priority.

After thinking for a while, I remember:

*"I think I was already working on this yesterday. I'll continue with this one."*

So I start working.

A little later, I discover that I cannot finish the task until I complete another task first.

So I switch to that task.

Then I discover that the second task also depends on something else.

Now I am doing a third task.

Then perhaps a fourth.

Several hours pass.

It is time for lunch.

After lunch, I come back, look at my todo list, and ask:

**What was I doing?**

The original task is still sitting there as "In Progress."

But I am actually working on a prerequisite of a prerequisite of that task.

So I try to remember the chain and continue.

Meanwhile, I have created several new todos to represent everything I discovered along the way.

At the end of the day, I look at the list again.

Many of the things I scheduled for today have not even been started.

I move them to tomorrow.

I tell myself:

*"I worked hard today. I tried my best."*

The next morning, I open the app again.

And the same questions return.

**What should I do now?**

**What progress did I actually make yesterday?**

**Why did I create all of these tasks?**

**How are they related?**

**What happened to the thing I originally wanted to achieve?**

For a day or two, I may still remember the answers.

After a few weeks, however, hundreds of items accumulate.

Then the list becomes chaos.

# The Problem Is Bigger Than Todo

A todo app usually represents work as a list of items:

**Todo → In Progress → Done**

But real life is rarely that simple.

Suppose I have a goal.

I spend weeks doing things related to it.

Then circumstances change.

Maybe I discover that my original plan was wrong.

Maybe I decide to pursue another approach.

Maybe the goal is no longer important.

What should I do with the goal?

Mark it as **Done**?

That would not be true.

Mark it as **Failed**?

That may not be true either.

Leave it **In Progress** forever?

Then I lose any meaningful understanding of what happened.

And if I simply delete or close it, all of the work, exploration, decisions, and lessons around it disappear from view.

The problem is that a flat todo list remembers the items, but it does not remember the **journey**.

It does not tell me:

* why a task existed,
* which goal it supported,
* what caused the plan to change,
* what I actually did,
* what I discovered along the way,
* which paths failed,
* which decisions changed my direction,
* or how all of these things relate to each other.

That is what I wanted to preserve.

# Life as a Map

I began imagining something different.

Instead of a todo list, what if I had a **map**?

On the map, I could see where I wanted to go.

I could see the route I planned to take.

I could see where I had actually gone.

I could see the detours.

I could see the wrong turns.

I could see why I changed direction.

And when I became lost, I would not have to reconstruct everything from memory.

I could look at the map.

That idea became **Becoming**.

Becoming tries to model not only **what you need to do**, but also the relationships between your intentions, plans, actions, experiences, and reflections.

# The Building Blocks of Becoming

Becoming represents pieces of your life through several connected concepts.

## Idea

An Idea is something that casually appears in your mind.

It does not need to be important or actionable yet.

For example:

> While driving home, I suddenly think, "I want a hamburger."

I can simply capture it.

Later, maybe my mom calls and reminds me that there is fresh meat and vegetables at home.

Now I may change my mind.

The original idea still existed, but something happened that influenced what I decided to do next.

## Goal

If I decide that I actually want to act on the idea, it may become a goal.

For example:

> **Goal: Get a hamburger before going home.**

Now there is somewhere I want to go.

The goal gives my actions a direction.

## Project

Sometimes reaching a goal is not a single action.

The hamburger shop might be far away.

I may need to decide which shop to visit, choose a route, stop somewhere else first, and think about how much time I am willing to spend.

A Project becomes the place where I organize how I intend to achieve the goal.

It represents the route I am currently trying to take.

## Task

The project can then contain concrete actions.

For example:

> Drive 10 km to the first stop.

> Buy some drinks.

> Turn left and continue toward the hamburger shop.

Tasks describe the specific actions I believe will move me along the route.

But during execution, reality may be different from the plan.

New tasks can appear.

Some tasks can become unnecessary.

The route can change.

Becoming should preserve those changes rather than pretending that the original plan happened exactly as expected.

## Record

A Record captures what actually happened.

Plans describe what I intended to do.

Records describe what I really did.

That distinction matters.

Over time, records allow me to compare:

**What did I plan?**

with

**What actually happened?**

## Note

Later that evening, I might reflect on the experience.

Maybe I realize:

> Going out of my way to buy a hamburger took too much time, and it was not particularly healthy. Next time, I should probably just go home and cook.

I can write this as a Note and connect it to the goal, project, tasks, and records that caused me to think about it.

The lesson is no longer an isolated sentence in a notebook.

It has context.

I can see **why I learned it**.

# What Becoming Is Trying to Do

Becoming is therefore not just another place to keep todos.

It is a place to connect:

**what you think,
what you want,
what you plan,
what you do,
what happens,
what you learn,
and why you change direction.**

Over time, these connections form a map.

A map of where you have been.

A map of where you are going.

And perhaps most importantly, a map that helps you understand **why you are going there at all**.

**Record what you do. Shape who you become.**
